# Grammar (G)

English grammar that a checker often reads past, because the sentence stays
clear enough to understand. Most of these errors come from the writer's first
language, also when the writer writes in English directly. A check can skip
this group, for example on an early draft that needs story and claims first.
Basic grammar that a checker always catches (*a/an*, subject–verb agreement,
sound-alikes such as *affect/effect*) has no rule here. All rules are
paraphrased; the sources are at the end.

### G1 Articles and uncountable nouns

**Rule**: A singular countable noun needs *a*, *an* or *the*; it cannot stand
alone. Use no article for a general plural or a general uncountable noun
(*Physical activity declines with age*), and *the* only for a specific item
the reader can identify (*the activity our respondents reported*).
Uncountable nouns (*information*, *knowledge*, *advice*, *equipment*,
*research*, *evidence*, *feedback*) take no plural and no *a/an*; use *much*,
*little* and *less* with them, and *many*, *few* and *fewer* with countable
nouns.
**Why**: The article tells the reader whether the text means one specific
item or any member of a class. Articles are the most common grammar problem
for writers whose first language has none, such as Czech or Polish. A wrong
article can also change the claim: *the coaches* is a known group, *coaches*
is coaches in general (S4).
**Example**:
✗ *We collected a research on coaches. The evidences show that the physical
activity declines with age.*
✓ *We collected research on coaches. The evidence shows that physical
activity declines with age.*
**Applies**: every section.

### G2 Fixed prepositions

**Rule**: Use the preposition English requires: *for* with a duration and
*since* with a start point (*for 5 years*, not *since 5 years*); *in* 2024,
*on* Monday, *at* noon; fixed verb pairs (*consist of*, *focus on*, *depend
on*, *differ from*, *participate in*).
**Why**: Prepositions follow fixed pairs that a writer learns one by one, and
they often differ from the writer's first language (German *seit* covers both
*for* and *since*).
**Example**:
✗ *Respondents coached since 5 years and participated on the survey in
Monday.*
✓ *Respondents had coached for 5 years and took part in the survey on
Monday.*
**Applies**: every section.

### G3 Verb + object, -ing or infinitive

**Rule**: Some verbs need an object before the infinitive (*allow*, *advise*,
*enable*, *force*, *remind*, *tell*): *allowed us to compare* or *allowed a
comparison*, never *allowed to compare*. Some take the *-ing* form (*avoid*,
*consider*, *suggest*, *recommend*, *finish*, *risk*, *delay*): *we suggest
using*, not *we suggest to use*. Some take the infinitive (*decide*, *plan*,
*intend*, *attempt*, *expect*, *hope*, *need*, *agree*).
**Why**: Each verb takes one form after it, and English has no general rule
for which. Writers whose first language is not English often use the
infinitive everywhere. *Allowed to compare* also hides who was allowed.
**Example**:
✗ *The design allowed to compare the groups, so we suggest to use it.*
✓ *The design allowed us to compare the groups, so we suggest using it.*
**Applies**: every section.

### G4 Commas

**Rule**: Put a comma after an opening clause, a long opening phrase, or a
connector that starts the sentence (*However,*); a short opening phrase needs
none. Put no comma before a *that*-clause the sentence needs, including after
reporting verbs (*found that*, *showed that*, *believe that*). Put no comma
between a subject and its verb, even after a long subject, between two verbs
with one subject, or before a final *because* or *if* clause unless there is
a sharp contrast.
**Why**: In English the comma marks where the main clause starts; a comma
elsewhere cuts the sentence where the grammar does not break. Many languages
always put a comma before their word for *that* (Czech *že*, German *dass*),
and writers carry it into English.
**Example**:
✗ *However most coaches reported, that they planned sessions weekly.*
✓ *However, most coaches reported that they planned sessions weekly.*
✗ *Coaches with more than 10 years of experience in youth sport, reported less
planning.*
✓ *Coaches with more than 10 years of experience in youth sport reported less
planning.*
**Applies**: every section.

### G5 No comma splice

**Rule**: Do not join two independent clauses with a comma alone. Use a full
stop, a semicolon, or a comma plus *and*, *but* or *so*. *However* or
*therefore* between two clauses takes a semicolon or a full stop before it and
a comma after it.
**Why**: The reader expects the first clause to go on and must go back when a
new subject and verb arrive. Some languages allow a comma between two main
clauses, so the error transfers. The *however* case reads well aloud, so a
checker misses it most often.
**Example**:
✗ *Most coaches planned weekly, however, few wrote the plan down.*
✓ *Most coaches planned weekly; however, few wrote the plan down.*
✓ *Most coaches planned weekly, but few wrote the plan down.*
**Applies**: every section.

### G6 "That" or "which": commas as a claim

**Rule**: Use *that* without commas for a clause that limits the noun, and
*which* with commas for a clause that only adds information. Read a comma
pair as a claim: *Respondents, who used the app, reported …* says every
respondent used it. When the reading is not clear, ask the author.
**Why**: The commas tell the reader whether the clause picks a subgroup or
describes the whole group, so one comma pair turns *some* into *all* (S5). US
English and APA keep *that* for limiting clauses; UK English also allows
*which* without commas.
**Example**:
✗ *Coaches, who held a licence, reported more planning.* (Says all coaches
held a licence; wrong if only some did.)
✓ *Coaches who held a licence reported more planning.* (Only those with one.)
**Applies**: every section. When the text does not show whether the clause
covers everyone, the finding is a question, not an edit.

### G7 No dangling modifier

**Rule**: The actor of an opening phrase is the subject of the main clause.
Keep a participle phrase next to the noun it describes.
**Why**: Otherwise the sentence says something false: *Using a survey, data
were collected* says the data used the survey. The passive is the usual
cause, so the error is common in the Methods. A checker reads past it because
the context makes the meaning clear; reviewers do not.
**Example**:
✗ *Using a survey, data were collected from 300 coaches.*
✓ *Using a survey, we collected data from 300 coaches.*
✗ *To reduce bias, the raters were blinded.*
✓ *To reduce bias, we blinded the raters.*
**Applies**: every section; most often in the Methods.

### G8 Colon only after a full clause

**Rule**: Put a colon only after a complete clause; none directly after
*include*, *are*, *such as* or *e.g.*
**Why**: A colon tells the reader that a statement is finished and a list or
an explanation follows. After *include*, the sentence is not finished.
**Example**:
✗ *The survey items included: planning, load and recovery.*
✓ *The survey items included planning, load and recovery.*
✓ *The survey had three items: planning, load and recovery.*
**Applies**: every section.

### G9 Hyphen in a compound modifier

**Rule**: Hyphenate a compound modifier before its noun (*long-term effect*,
*10-year-old children*), not after it (*the effect was long term*).
**Why**: The two words act as one adjective only before the noun. Without the
hyphen the reader can group the words wrongly: *small club coaches* is small
coaches or coaches of small clubs.
**Example**:
✗ *a long term follow up of small club coaches*
✓ *a long-term follow-up of small-club coaches*
**Applies**: every section.

### G10 False friends

**Rule**: Check a word that looks like a word in the author's first language,
and make sure its English meaning is what the sentence needs.
**Why**: A familiar-looking word can mean something else in English. The
sentence stays good English, so a grammar check does not see the error. It
happens when a writer writes English directly too, because the writer still
thinks with the first language's words. EASE: "If in doubt, check the
definition in an English dictionary."
**Example**:
✗ *The actual guidelines recommend weekly planning.* (Czech *aktuální* means
*current*.)
✓ *The current guidelines recommend weekly planning.*
**Applies**: every section. No word list: the checker knows the common
pairs, and a list would tie the guide to one language. If the author's first
language is not recorded, the finding is a question: which meaning is
intended?
