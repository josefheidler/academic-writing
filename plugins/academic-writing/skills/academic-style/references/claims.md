# Claim strength (S)

A sentence claims exactly what its evidence shows: our data, or the source it
cites. Strength lives in small words (quantifiers, verbs, articles, hedges),
so an edit for style can change a claim without anyone noticing. These rules
come from refining real papers, and the Purdue OWL adds to some of them (see
Sources); S2 and S5 match the never-change lists of
professional editing services.

### S1 Superlatives and comparisons checked

**Rule**: Use *most*, *least*, *only*, *all*, *every*, *first*, *never* or a
comparison (*more than*, *the same as*) only when a check over the full set
supports it.
**Why**: A superlative is a claim about every member of a set, and it is often
checked against a subset (a table that left out small groups). An edit that
turns a comparison into a superlative (*more often* → *most often*) makes a
new claim. A bare plural with no share (*Respondents expected …*) reads as
*all respondents* and needs the same check.
**Example**:
✗ *Nurses reported the most overtime.* (Checked against three of seven
staff groups.)
✓ *Nurses reported more overtime than doctors or porters.*
**Applies**: every section; titles, highlights and figure titles too.

### S2 No causal word the evidence lacks

**Rule**: Use a causal word (*leads to*, *causes*, *drives*, *because*,
*therefore*, *so*, *due to*, *effect*) only when the design or the cited
source shows a cause. Never use *prove* or *proof* for an empirical result;
use *show*, *indicate*, *suggest* or *support*.
**Why**: A cross-sectional study shows an association. A causal verb, or a
causal connector between two findings, claims a mechanism that no one
measured. Reviewers catch it, and readers believe it. One study cannot
establish a theory, and a statistical test supports a claim but does not
prove it.
**Example**:
✗ *Screen time leads to shorter sleep.* (Survey data.)
✓ *More screen time was associated with shorter sleep.*
✗ *Our data prove that licensed coaches plan more.*
✓ *Our data show that licensed coaches planned more often than coaches
without a licence.*
**Applies**: every section; also connectors added for flow (C4). A *so* or
*therefore* that joins two steps of the authors' reasoning (*we did not
measure X, so other explanations remain*) claims no cause in the data.

### S3 Words match what was measured

**Rule**: Use the verb and the time the measure or the question used: *use*,
*want*, *expect* and *trust* are different claims; so are *now* and *in
future*, and *estimated* and *measured*. Name the measure: *mean*, *median*
or *mode*, not *average*.
**Why**: A survey that asked what people use cannot show what they prefer. A
reworded sentence that reads better often drifts from what was asked, and
that drift is invisible to a number check. *Average* does not say which
measure it is, and in skewed data (training hours) the mean and the median
differ widely.
**Example**:
✗ *Most patients prefer video visits.* (The question asked which visits they
had.)
✓ *Most patients had at least one video visit.*
✗ *On average, coaches trained 6 hours per week.*
✓ *Coaches trained a median of 6 hours per week (IQR 4–9).*
**Applies**: every section; most often in the Results, the Discussion, the
Abstract and figure titles. Also the Methods sentence that describes a
question, and every rewording of a sentence that reports data.

### S4 Scope matches the sample

**Rule**: Name the group the data come from: the respondents, the sample,
this region, this setting. Widen the scope only with evidence.
**Why**: *Respondents* is a fact; *researchers*, *the field* or *people*
is a generalisation. A laboratory result stated without its setting reads as
free-living.
**Example**:
✗ *Teachers distrust the new curriculum.*
✓ *Most teachers in our survey distrusted the new curriculum.*
**Applies**: every section; most often Abstract, Discussion, Conclusion.

### S5 Edits keep strength

**Rule**: An edit keeps the claim's strength unless the author asks to change
it. When an edit changes a hedge, a quantifier, an article or a scope, say so
in the finding.
**Why**: Polish works on small words, and small words carry strength. *Some*
→ *the*, *may* → *does*, *in our sample* → nothing: each reads as style and
is a new claim. *The parents agreed* says all of them did.
**Example**:
Author: *Some of the parents were in agreement.*
✗ Proposal: *The parents agreed.* (Shorter, but now every parent agreed.)
✓ Proposal: *Some parents agreed.* (Shorter, and the strength is the same.)
**Applies**: every edit, by the author or by you.

### S6 Each clause supported

**Rule**: In a sentence with a citation, each clause needs support: from that
source, from another cited source, or from our data. A clause with none gets
its own source or goes. A plural (*studies*, *surveys*) needs more than one
citation; with one source, say *one study*, or add the others.
**Why**: Authors add a clause to a cited sentence, and the citation at the end
seems to cover it. Readers assume it does. *Studies have shown* tells the
reader that several sources agree.
**Example**:
✗ *Sleep loss impairs memory and is now common among students [@smith2020].*
(The source covers memory only.)
✓ *Sleep loss impairs memory [@smith2020].*
✗ *Studies have shown that coaches underestimate load [@lee2020].*
✓ *One study found that coaches underestimate load [@lee2020].*
**Applies**: every cited sentence; again after each edit to it. A general
claim stated as fact with no citation and no data of ours (*measurement
matters less than interpretation*) is a finding too: it needs a source, or a
hedge that marks it as our view.

### S7 Name the comparator

**Rule**: Every comparison names both sides and the base of each: *higher
than whom*, *of whom*. A difference also names its direction: which side is
higher. When the text does not show it, ask the author.
**Why**: *Higher than the rest* hides whether *the rest* is everyone, the
same subgroup, or another study. A dropped condition can keep the numbers
and change the comparison.
**Example**:
✗ *Night-shift nurses reported more back pain (45%).*
✓ *Night-shift nurses reported more back pain than day-shift nurses (45% vs
30%).*
✗ *Training volume differed between female and male players.*
✓ *Female players trained more than male players (U = 812, p = .02).*
**Applies**: every section; most often in the Results, the Discussion and
figure titles. A comparator the sentence makes obvious (*older adults slept
longer; younger adults napped more*) counts as named.

### S8 Evaluative words need their number

**Rule**: Give an evaluative adjective or adverb (*large*, *high*,
*substantial*, *clearly*, *markedly*) its number, or cut it. A directional
verb (*increased*, *improved*, *reduced*, *declined*) also gets its amount.
**Why**: Without the number, the adjective is the claim, and the reader cannot
judge it. Without its number, *clearly* or *markedly* in Results also reads as
a tested difference. A directional verb gives the direction but not the size:
1 point or 30?
**Example**:
✗ *Adherence was clearly higher in the morning group.*
✓ *Adherence was higher in the morning group (81% vs 64%).*
✗ *Weekly planning increased after the course.*
✓ *Weekly planning increased from 42% to 61% after the course.*
**Applies**: every section; most often in the Results, the Abstract and the
Discussion. With the number beside it, an adverb is not a finding.

### S9 "Significant" only for a test

**Rule**: Use *significant* only for the result of a statistical test. For
"important" or "large", use those words.
**Why**: In everyday English *significant* means *important*; in a paper,
readers take it as a test result. Without a test, it claims one.
**Example**:
✗ *A significant share of clinics had no protocol.* (No test.)
✓ *Two in five clinics had no protocol.*
**Applies**: every section.

### S10 Points or percent

**Rule**: For a change between two percentages, say whether it is in
percentage points or relative percent.
**Why**: A rise from 20% to 30% is 10 percentage points and 50% in relative
terms. *Rose by 10%* can mean either, and the two differ by a factor of five.
**Example**:
✗ *Screening rose by 10% (from 20% to 30%).*
✓ *Screening rose by 10 percentage points (from 20% to 30%).*
**Applies**: every section; most often in the Results, the Discussion and the
Abstract.

### S11 A source's words in quotation marks

**Rule**: Put a source's exact phrase in quotation marks, with its page.
Otherwise, say it in your own words. A paraphrase changes the sentence
structure, not only some words: a near-copy with a few words changed
(patchwriting) is quoted or rewritten in your own structure.
**Why**: A distinctive phrase taken without quotation marks presents the
source's words as ours, and a reader or a similarity check sees it. OWL
counts patchwriting as plagiarism even with a citation. Writers whose first
language is not English do it more often, because the source sentence is
already good English.
**Example**:
✗ *The review called school sport an underused lever for health
[@park2021].* (The source's words.)
✓ *The review called school sport "an underused lever for health"
[@park2021, p. 4].*
✗ *School sport is an underutilised tool for population health
[@park2021].* (Source: *School sport is an underused lever for public
health.* Same structure, three words changed.)
✓ *Park (2021) argued that schools could do more for public health through
sport.*
**Applies**: every section: every cited sentence, and a sentence with no
citation that uses a source's words.

### S12 Reporting verb matches the source

**Rule**: Choose the reporting verb for what the source did: *found* or
*showed* for data, *argued* or *proposed* for a claim, *suggested* for a
hedged claim. Use an opposing verb (*rejected*, *disputed*) only when the
source disagrees.
**Why**: The verb tells the reader how strong the source's evidence is.
*Showed* for a source that only argued adds support it does not have. S6
checks that a clause has support, not how the text describes it.
**Example**:
✗ *Lee (2020) showed that coaches underestimate load.* (An opinion paper.)
✓ *Lee (2020) argued that coaches underestimate load.*
**Applies**: every cited sentence, in every section. The check needs the
source or the reference ledger.

### S13 "Only", "just" and "even" next to what they limit

**Rule**: Put *only*, *just* or *even* directly before the word or phrase it
limits. When the position allows two readings, ask the author.
**Why**: These words limit what comes right after them, so their position is
part of the claim. A style edit that moves the word changes the claim (S5).
**Example**:
✗ *We only surveyed coaches in 2024.* (No other group, or no other year?)
✓ *We surveyed only coaches in 2024.* (No other group.)
✓ *We surveyed coaches only in 2024.* (No other year.)
**Applies**: every section.
