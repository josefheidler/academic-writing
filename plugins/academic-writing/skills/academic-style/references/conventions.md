# Academic conventions (A)

The default is APA 7. Where APA is silent, EASE decides. The journal's
instructions for authors override both. All rules are paraphrased; the
sources are at the end.

### A1 Tense by section

**Rule**: Use the past tense for what was done and found (Methods, Results,
a specific earlier study). Use the present tense for established knowledge,
interpretation, and what a figure or table shows. Keep one tense through a
paragraph unless the time changes. Use the present perfect for a past action
that continues or matters now, including a body of literature (*researchers
have found*); use no present perfect with a finished time (*in 2019*, *3
years ago*).
**Why**: Tense tells the reader whose claim it is and how settled it is.
*Children slept less* is this study's result; *children sleep less* is a
general fact. A tense switch in mid-paragraph makes a result read as a
general truth. *Has shown in 2019* says both "over" and "still true". Many
languages have no tense that works like the English present perfect, so
writers put it in the wrong places.
**Example**:
✗ (Results) *Older pupils skip breakfast more often than younger ones.*
✓ (Results) *Older pupils skipped breakfast more often than younger ones.*
✓ (Discussion) *Breakfast habits change with age* (with a citation).
✗ *Kim et al. have shown in 2019 that coaches plan weekly.*
✓ *Kim et al. (2019) showed that coaches plan weekly.*
✓ *Several studies have shown that coaches plan weekly.*
**Applies**: every section. Present tense for an established fact in the
Introduction is correct; do not change it to past.

### A2 One term per concept

**Rule**: Name each concept with one term. Define it at first use, then
repeat the same term. Where the article has a glossary or term lock, use its
terms.
**Why**: A new word tells the reader there is a new thing. *Sleep time*,
*sleep duration* and *time in bed* are three measures to a reader, even when
the author means one.
**Example**:
✗ *Participants wore the tracker for a week. The device recorded …*
✓ *Participants wore the tracker for a week. The tracker recorded …*
**Applies**: every section, across sections, and in figure labels and
captions. A change of term is a finding for the whole paper, not one
sentence: count every use before proposing it.

### A3 One spelling variant

**Rule**: Use UK or US spelling throughout, as the journal asks. Keep the
original spelling inside quotations and source titles.
**Why**: Mixed spelling reads as careless editing, and journals ask for one
variant.
**Example**: *behaviour … color* → *behaviour … colour* (UK).
**Applies**: every section.

### A4 Numbers and bases

**Rule**: Write numerals for 10 and above, and for every number with a unit,
percentage, statistic, age, score or sample size. Write zero to nine in words
otherwise. Spell out a number that starts a sentence, heading or title, or
better, reorder the sentence. Use % only after a numeral. Give the base of
every percentage once, where the group is named, and never beside a clause
whose count it is not. Use a decimal point, never a decimal comma, and a
comma as the thousands separator from 1,000 (APA writes page numbers, degrees
of freedom, temperatures and serial numbers without it). Write a range as
*from 10 to 20*, *between 10 and 20*, or with an en dash (*10–20*); never
with a hyphen, and never mixed (*from 10–20*).
**Why**: Numerals let the reader scan the numbers that matter. A base in the
wrong place changes what it counts: *40% were smokers (n = 120)* reads as 120
smokers. In English, *2,500* is two thousand five hundred, so a writer from a
decimal-comma language who writes *2,5* shows the reader a number 1,000 times
larger. *From 10–20* says "to" twice.
**Example**:
✗ *Of the participants, 40% were smokers (n = 120).*
✓ *Of the 120 participants, 40% were smokers.*
✗ *Coaches trained 2,5 hours per session; 1500 respondents aged from 25-40
took part.*
✓ *Coaches trained 2.5 hours per session; 1,500 respondents aged from 25 to
40 took part.*
**Applies**: every section, tables and captions. Time is a unit (*5
years*). A base given earlier in the paragraph or section counts: look there
before raising a finding, and if the earlier text is not available, ask. If the paper already writes a case one way
throughout, raise it once, for the whole paper. APA 7 has more cases
(ordinals, very large numbers); look them up when one comes up.

### A5 Abbreviations

**Rule**: Define an abbreviation at its first use in the text, and again at
first use in the abstract and in each table or figure note. Abbreviate only a
term used often (APA: three times or more), and keep standard ones (*SD*,
*CI*) undefined. Plurals of abbreviations and numbers take no apostrophe
(*SDs*, *1990s*); units take no plural *s* (*12 m*). Abbreviate a unit only
after a numeral (*15 kg*, but *several kilograms*), and never abbreviate
*day*, *week*, *month* or *year*. Latin abbreviations take periods (*e.g.*,
*i.e.*, *et al.*); capital-letter ones take none (*MSc*, *URL*).
**Why**: A reader who starts at the Results or the figure has not seen the
first definition. An abbreviation used twice costs the reader more than it
saves. *SD's* reads as a possessive, and *12 ms* is milliseconds.
**Example**: *moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA)* at first use,
*MVPA* after.
✗ *The SD's were 4.2 and 5.1; coaches trained 3 hrs per wk.*
✓ *The SDs were 4.2 and 5.1; coaches trained 3 hours per week.*
**Applies**: every section; abstract and captions count apart.

### A6 Bias-free language

**Rule**: Describe people with specific, respectful terms: *participants* or
*respondents*, not *subjects*; person-first or identity-first as the group
prefers; no labels that reduce a person to a condition. Use gender-neutral
pronouns: no generic *he*, and no *he/she*, *(s)he* or *s/he*; use a plural,
rephrase, or use singular *they*.
**Why**: A label such as *the diabetics* names a person by a condition, and
readers in that group read it as disrespect. A specific term such as
*respondents* also tells the reader what role the person had in the study. A
generic *he* leaves out part of the readers; slash forms distract.
**Example**: *the diabetics* → *people with diabetes*.
✗ *Each coach reported how often he/she planned a session.*
✓ *Each coach reported how often they planned a session.*
**Applies**: every section. Flag; the author and the journal decide the
term.

### A7 One hedge per claim

**Rule**: Hedge a claim once, with the word that matches the evidence. Do not
stack hedges.
**Why**: One hedge marks a claim as uncertain. Two or three (*may possibly
suggest*) make it say nothing, and readers cannot tell how sure the authors
are.
**Example**:
✗ *These results may possibly suggest a potential link.*
✓ *These results suggest a link.* or *There may be a link.*
**Applies**: every section; most often in the Discussion, the Conclusion and
the Abstract. A number we measured takes no hedge (*about 40%* for a measured
41% is a finding). A change to a hedge is also a change of claim strength:
see S5.

### A8 "We" for the authors

**Rule**: Use *we* for what the authors did, not *the authors* or *the
researchers*. Make sure *we* covers only the people who did it. Never use *we*
for people in general.
**Why**: *We* is short and clear, and APA prefers it. A *we* that credits all
authors with one author's act, or that means "everyone", says something
untrue.
**Example**:
✗ *The authors measured blood pressure twice.*
✓ *We measured blood pressure twice.*
✗ *We all sit too much.*
✓ *Many adults sit for most of the day* (with a citation).
**Applies**: every section.

### A9 Passive for the unknown actor

**Rule**: Use the active voice when the actor matters. Use the passive when
the actor is unknown, obvious or irrelevant.
**Why**: The passive hides who acted. That is right for a procedure (*samples
were frozen*) and wrong when the actor is the point (*it was decided*).
**Example**:
✗ *It was decided to exclude late arrivals.* (Who decided?)
✓ *We excluded late arrivals.*
✓ *Samples were stored at −80 °C.* (No finding: the actor does not matter.)
**Applies**: every section. A passive where the actor does not matter is
not a finding.

### A10 "This study" is ours

**Rule**: Use *this study* or *the present study* only for our own study. For
a cited study, use *that study*, *their study*, or the citation.
**Why**: In a Discussion that moves between our results and others', *this
study* after a citation makes the reader ask whose result it is.
**Example**:
✗ *Kim et al. surveyed 300 teachers. This study found low uptake.*
✓ *Kim et al. surveyed 300 teachers and found low uptake.*
**Applies**: every section; most often in the Introduction and the
Discussion.

### A11 Human verbs only for humans

**Rule**: Give human verbs only to people. A study, table or dataset does not
*control*, *interpret*, *believe* or *attempt*; *the results indicate* and
*Table 2 shows* are correct. Use *who* for people and *that* or *which* for
things.
**Why**: In APA the researchers act, not the study. *The study controlled for
age* hides who made the choice. APA 7 also bars *who* for things.
**Example**:
✗ *The survey attempted to measure planning, and the model controlled for
age.*
✓ *We used the survey to measure planning, and we controlled for age in the
model.*
**Applies**: every section; most often in the Methods and the Results. See
also A8.

### A12 Numbered labels take a capital

**Rule**: A noun followed by its number is a label and takes a capital:
*Table 2*, *Figure 1*, *Appendix B*, *Item 4*. Without the number, write the
plain word: *the table*, *the fourth item*.
**Why**: APA treats a numbered element as a name; the capital tells the reader
that the text points to one element of the paper.
**Example**:
✗ *As figure 2 shows, item 4 had the lowest score (see table 1).*
✓ *As Figure 2 shows, Item 4 had the lowest score (see Table 1).*
**Applies**: every section and captions.

### A13 No contractions, "you", or questions to the reader

**Rule**: Write no contractions (*do not*, *it is*). Do not address the reader
as *you* or give the reader commands. Ask the reader no questions; a research
question that the text then answers is correct.
**Why**: Contractions are informal. *You* has no clear audience: the reader,
the coaches, or people in general? Commands and direct questions belong to
speech. They are rare in an author's text and common in model drafts.
**Example**:
✗ *Coaches don't plan weekly. Why is this? Consider how little time you have
after work.*
✓ *Coaches did not plan weekly. The most common reason they gave was a lack of
time after work.*
**Applies**: every section. A quotation keeps its own words.

### A14 Statistics in APA form

**Rule**: Use a leading zero only for values that can exceed 1 (*M* = 0.52,
but *p* = .03, *r* = .45). Give *M* and *SD* to one decimal; *r*,
proportions and test statistics to two; an exact *p* to two or three, and
*p* < .001 below that. Use words in running text (*the mean*) and symbols in
parentheses or next to =; italicise *M*, *SD*, *N*, *n*, *p* and *t*; *N* is
the whole sample and *n* a subgroup. Give a confidence interval with its level
and its limits in brackets: *95% CI [1.2, 3.4]*.
**Why**: This is the APA form, and readers compare numbers more easily when
the form never changes.
**Example**:
✗ *The M was 4.21 (SD 1.08), r = 0.45, p = 0.000.*
✓ *The mean was 4.2 (SD = 1.1), r = .45, p < .001.*
**Applies**: every section, tables and captions.
