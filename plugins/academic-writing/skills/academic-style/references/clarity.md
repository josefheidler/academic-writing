# Clarity and story (C)

How a reader moves through a sentence, a paragraph and a section. C1–C2 come
from Gopen and Swan (1990), paraphrased. The rest come from EASE and from
refining real papers.

### C1 Subject and verb early

**Rule**: Put the grammatical subject near the start of the sentence and its
verb right after it.
**Why**: Until the verb arrives, the reader holds every word in memory and
does not know what the sentence claims. A long gap between subject and verb
is the commonest cause of a re-read, more so for readers whose first language
is not English.
**Example**:
✗ *The children who slept less than 8 hours on school nights during the
2 winter months of the study scored lower.*
✓ *The children scored lower when they slept less than 8 hours on school
nights during the 2 winter months.*
**Applies**: every section.

### C2 Old before new

**Rule**: Start a sentence with what the reader already knows (the topic
position) and end it on the new point (the stress position).
**Why**: The start links the sentence to the one before; the end is where the
reader puts the weight. New information at the start and a known term at the
end hide the point and break the link.
**Example**:
✗ *A drop in reaction time of 40 ms followed caffeine.*
✓ *After caffeine, reaction time dropped by 40 ms.*
**Applies**: every section; most useful in Results and Discussion.

### C3 One idea per paragraph

**Rule**: Give each paragraph one idea, state it in the first sentence, and
end the paragraph where the idea ends.
**Why**: Readers skim first sentences. A paragraph with two ideas buries the
second one, and a paragraph without a lead sentence makes the reader find the
idea themselves.
**Example**:
✗ *Most coaches planned their sessions weekly (62%). Weekly planning was more
common among coaches with a higher licence. After matches, most coaches gave
their players a rest day.* (The third sentence starts a new idea.)
✓ The third sentence opens a new paragraph on its own idea: *Recovery after
matches followed one pattern: most coaches gave their players a rest day.*
**Applies**: every section. A Methods paragraph may hold one procedure. A
paragraph of one or two sentences is a proposal to merge it with the
paragraph it belongs to; a short paragraph for a transition or for emphasis
is often right, and the author decides.

### C4 Link each sentence to the next

**Rule**: Join each sentence to the one before it with a repeated term, a
time, a contrast, or one plain connector (*also*, *but*, *so*, *then*, *in
contrast*).
**Why**: Prose reads as a story when the reader sees why each sentence comes
next. A list of unlinked facts makes the reader build the links, and some
build the wrong ones.
**Example**:
✗ *Response rates fell in the second wave. Coaches over 50 answered least
often.*
✓ *Response rates fell in the second wave. The fall was largest among coaches
over 50.* (The repeated idea, *fall*, is the link; no connector is added.)
**Applies**: every section. A link that claims a cause (*so*, *therefore*,
*because*) also needs S2.

### C5 Say it once

**Rule**: Say each point once, at the place the reader needs it. Cut a
sentence that repeats the one before, a definition the reader already has, or
a detail the figure or table already shows.
**Why**: A short paper has no room for a second telling, and every repeat
makes the reader ask what changed. Authors cut overexplaining on first read;
draft lean and offer the detail as an option.
**Example**:
✗ *Table 2 shows the scores by school. As Table 2 shows, scores differed by
school, with School A highest.*
✓ *Scores differed by school, with School A highest (Table 2).*
**Applies**: every section. Across sections, a short summary in the
Conclusion of what the Discussion said is a repeat to flag, not to cut
silently.

### C6 Pronoun target

**Rule**: Make every pronoun and every bare *this*, *these* or *it* point to
one noun the reader can name, in the same number and person. After any edit,
check the pronouns of the sentences that follow it. A switch to *you* is
A13.
**Why**: An inserted or deleted sentence silently moves what *they* or *this*
points to. The sentence stays grammatical and says something else. A pronoun
in the wrong number (*it* for a plural) sends the reader to look for another
noun.
**Example**:
✗ *Teachers rated the pupils, and parents rated them too. They were
stricter.* (Who: teachers or parents?)
✓ *Teachers rated the pupils, and parents rated them too. The parents were
stricter.*
✗ *Each club sent their plan.* ✓ *Each club sent its plan.* (Singular *they*
for a person is correct: A6.)
**Applies**: every section; always after an insertion or a deletion.

### C7 One job per sentence

**Rule**: Split a sentence that does two jobs. Keep each qualifier attached
to the clause it limits.
**Why**: A sentence that both reports and interprets, or joins two findings,
makes the reader sort the parts. A careless split moves a qualifier (*in
boys*, *at baseline*) to the wrong half.
**Example**:
✗ *Attendance fell after the first term in the older year groups, which
suggests that the novelty wore off.*
✓ *Attendance fell after the first term in the older year groups. This drop
suggests that the novelty wore off.*
**Applies**: every section. Splitting for its own sake breaks T10. A
qualifier that can limit two parts of one sentence (*in both waves*: of the
measure, or of the change?) is a finding even without a split.

### C8 Counts that add up

**Rule**: In one sentence, give counts or groups that do not overlap, or say
that they overlap. Keep two equal numbers apart, or say which is which.
**Why**: Readers add counts that sit side by side. *Two nurses and three
night-shift staff* reads as five people, even when the nurses work nights.
Two equal numbers next to each other (*60% and 60%*) make the reader check
which belongs where.
**Example**:
✗ *Two surgeons and four staff with 10 years' experience refused.*
✓ *Five staff refused, among them two surgeons; four had 10 years'
experience.*
**Applies**: every section; most often in the sample description, the
Results, the Abstract and captions. Two groups overlap too: *managers and
staff over 50* may share people.

### C9 Plain word, concrete noun

**Rule**: Use the short common word (*use*, *enough*, *about*) and the
concrete noun for people and things (*nurses*, *parents*), not the long or
general one (*utilise*, *sufficient*, *stakeholders*).
**Why**: A long word where a short one fits slows the reader, and readers
judge its writer as less able (Oppenheimer, 2006). A general noun makes the
reader translate it back into the people meant.
**Example**:
✗ *Stakeholders utilised the portal to a sufficient extent.*
✓ *Parents used the portal enough.*
**Applies**: every section. A technical term with no plain equivalent is not
a finding. The words here are a language choice; what a paper explains is the
author's choice.

### C10 Action in a verb

**Rule**: Put the action in a verb, and name who acts when it matters.
Flag an abstract noun that hides a verb (*performed an analysis of* →
*analysed*).
**Why**: A hidden verb needs *was*, *of* and a passive to carry it, and the
sentence grows longer and vaguer. Keep the noun when it names a known concept
or refers back to the sentence before; noun-heavy text is not always harder
for non-native readers, so the author decides.
**Example**:
✗ *An assessment of the effectiveness of the reminders was carried out.*
✓ *We assessed whether the reminders worked.*
**Applies**: every section; flag, do not convert in bulk.

### C11 No noun stacks

**Rule**: Rewrite three or more nouns in a row as a phrase with a verb or a
preposition.
**Why**: The reader must work out which noun modifies which. In a stack like
*school meal uptake monitoring tool* the order of the parts is the meaning,
and readers whose first language is not English rebuild it word by word.
**Example**:
✗ *the school meal uptake monitoring tool*
✓ *the tool that monitors how many pupils take school meals*
**Applies**: every section. A fixed technical term (*heart rate monitor*) is
not a finding.

### C12 Parallel form

**Rule**: Give parallel content the same grammatical form: two reasons, two
groups or two findings in the same syntax and order. Keep one voice across
parallel clauses: no switch from active to passive halfway.
**Why**: When the form repeats, the reader sees at once what is being
compared. A change of form makes the reader ask whether the second item is a
different kind of thing.
**Example**:
✗ *Boys walked more; as for girls, cycling was what they did more often.*
✓ *Boys walked more; girls cycled more.*
✗ *We recruited coaches through clubs, and the survey was sent by email.*
✓ *We recruited coaches through clubs and sent the survey by email.*
**Applies**: every section; most useful in Results. The voice switch is most
common in the Methods.

### C13 No filler

**Rule**: Cut words that carry no content. The common cases:
- frames: *it is important to note that*, *it should be mentioned that*, and
  *it was found that X* → *X*;
- empty openings: *There are four items that measured load* → *Four items
  measured load*; keep one when the delay is the point;
- circumlocutions: *in order to* → *to*, *the fact that* → *that*, *due to the
  fact that* → *because*, *has the ability to* → *can*, *with regard to* →
  *about*, *at this point in time* → *now*, *in a situation in which* →
  *when*;
- redundant pairs: *end result*, *past history*, *each individual*, *period
  of time*, *large in size*, *completely eliminate*;
- empty modifiers, unless they restrict: *kind of*, *sort of*, *really*,
  *basically*, *actually*, *definitely*, *generally*, *particular*,
  *specific*, *individual*;
- double negatives: *few did not complete* → *almost all completed*.
**Why**: Filler delays the subject (C1) and tells the reader that something
matters without saying why. If it matters, the position of the sentence says
so. A main clause carries the weight, so a point behind a frame gets lost,
and two negatives make the reader decode the claim step by step.
**Example**:
✗ *It is important to note that, in order to reduce bias, the raters were
blinded.*
✓ *To reduce bias, we blinded the raters.*
✗ *Coaches basically planned really short sessions.*
✓ *Coaches planned short sessions (median 40 min).*
**Applies**: every section. Some cuts change strength, so they need S5:
*it is possible that* → *may* changes a hedge; *individual* in *individual
sports* (not team sports) restricts, so it stays; *few did not* → *most* is a
weaker claim and *not uncommon* → *common* a stronger one, so ask the author.

### C14 Cite the finding, not the author

**Rule**: Make the finding the subject of the sentence and put the citation
after it. Keep *Smith et al. found …* for a study the paragraph discusses in
detail.
**Why**: A chain of *Study A found … Study B found …* reads as a list of
papers, and the reader must build the argument. A finding as the subject
tells the story; the citations support it.
**Example**:
✗ *Lee et al. found that naps improved recall. Ortiz et al. also found this.*
✓ *Naps improved recall in two trials [@lee2019; @ortiz2021].*
**Applies**: Introduction and Discussion. In the Methods, *Kim et al.
developed the scale* gives credit for a method and is correct.

### C15 Finding first, statistic last

**Rule**: State the finding in plain words first and put the statistic in
parentheses at the end. Do not open the sentence with the test name.
**Why**: A sentence that opens with the test makes the reader get through the
method before the finding. The finding is the point; the statistic supports
it.
**Example**:
✗ *A chi-square test of independence showed a significant association between
licence level and weekly planning, χ²(2) = 9.4, p = .009.*
✓ *Coaches with a higher licence planned weekly more often than coaches with a
lower licence (χ²(2) = 9.4, p = .009).*
**Applies**: every section; most often in the Results and the Abstract.

### C16 Idioms are a flag

**Rule**: Flag an idiom, slang or a stock metaphor (*tip of the iceberg*,
*double-edged sword*, *a perfect storm*) and offer a plain form; the author
decides. Figurative language that explains something is not a finding. In
your own text, use none.
**Why**: Readers whose first language is not English may not know an idiom,
and APA discourages poetic devices. A well-placed idiom can also be the
author's voice, so in the author's text the plain form is an option, never an
edit.
**Example**:
✗ *Low planning rates are only the tip of the iceberg.*
✓ (option) *Low planning rates were one of four gaps that coaches reported.*
**Applies**: every section.
